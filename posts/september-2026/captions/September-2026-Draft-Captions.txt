When you explain the answer to your student, they learn the answer, but when you help them find the answer, they learn how to think!

Most parents who help with homework are accidentally making their student worse at working independently, not because they're doing anything wrong, but because explaining is faster than guiding.

The shift is simple: instead of showing them how, ask questions.

"What do you know about this problem so far?" "What's the part that's confusing you?" "What would happen if you tried it this way?"

Those questions don't give them the answer, they give them a thinking process they can use when you're not in the room.

That's the difference between help that helps and help that creates dependency!

If your student is spending two or more hours on homework every night, something is off.

Not because effort is bad, but because two hours is usually the point where frustration starts replacing learning.

Any of those three needs to be addressed, not extended.

=== 2026-09-17 | IG | Parent Tips ===

[ CAPTION ]
When your student brings home a grade that disappoints them, your first instinct might be to ask why or to express concern.

Both of those usually backfire.

If you immediately ask "why did this happen?" your student hears judgment. They get defensive. The conversation shuts down before it starts.

Instead, start with curiosity, not evaluation: "That's lower than you wanted. Walk me through what happened." Let them tell the story first.

Most of the time, your student already knows why the grade happened. They don't need your analysis, they need to feel heard about their disappointment.

After they've explained, ask what they want to do about it: "What do you need to do differently next time? Do you need any help with that?"

That shifts the conversation from shame to problem-solving. Your student stays engaged instead of shutting down.

Grades matter, but your relationship with your student matters more. How you talk about them shapes whether your student trusts you with the next disappointment.

Twenty-five minutes of focused work, then a five-minute break.

That's the Pomodoro Technique, and it works because it matches how your brain actually operates.

Your brain has a focus window. After about 20 to 30 minutes of sustained attention, your capacity for focus starts dropping. Your mind wanders. You start re-reading the same paragraph. You're going through the motions but not actually processing.

A five-minute break lets your brain reset. When you come back, your focus capacity resets with it.

One caveat: the break can't be your phone. The break has to actually rest your attention. Walk to the kitchen. Look out the window. Do stretches. Anything that lets your brain shift gears.

Phone scrolling doesn't rest your attention, it just moves it to a different thing. When the timer goes off, you come back more tired, not refreshed.

If your student's study sessions feel like they're slowing down after thirty minutes, this is why. The timer and the real break both matter.

=== 2026-09-29 | IG | Test Prep ===

[ CAPTION ]
Taking one more full-length practice test three days before the real test doesn't help.

This is counterintuitive, but the research is clear: at that point, another test isn't building learning, it's building fatigue.

Your brain needs recovery time. The learning from a full test doesn't happen the day you take it, it happens after when you sleep and your brain consolidates what you learned.

If you take a full test on Tuesday and the real test is Friday, you're showing up tired from the practice test and you haven't had time for consolidation.

The better play: finish your full practice tests a week or more before the real test. Take this last week to review your weak question types, drilling the specific ones that cost you points, not retaking another full test.

Then rest the three days before so your brain shows up fresh and consolidated.

Your last full test isn't your final preparation, it's your roadmap. Link in bio.
